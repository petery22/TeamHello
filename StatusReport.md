# Status Report
## Project Overview
This project examines how artist records overlap across three of the major US museums: the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Museum of Modern Art, and the National Gallery of Art. Using the museums’ open-access data, we acquired, verified, cleaned, and assessed the datasets and will integrate artist records using ULAN IDs, Wikidata QIDs, and normalized names. Our goal is to measure overlapping artists across museums and identify any disagreements between them.

## Task Progress Update
1. Download the data (Complete by Andy): All three datasets are downloaded from the official GitHub repository of the museums and verified with SHA-256 checksums. The script “scripts/download_data.py” downloads “MetObjects.csv”, “Artists.csv”, and “constituents.csv” into a local data folder and checks each file against its expected checksum. For documentation, “docs/data_documentation.md” records the source, license, size, checksum, and known issues of each dataset. Then, “scripts/assess_data.py” measures how complete the raw data is and how many artists have Wikidata or ULAN identifiers, and the results are in “results/completeness.csv”. The raw data folder is then excluded from GitHub because the Met file is too large at about 341 megabytes. A key finding through this task is that NGA has the best identifier coverage, with 66% of artists having a Wikidata or ULAN ID compared to 36% for the Met and 20% for MoMA. Since most artists have no identifier, it makes name-based matching important.
2. Check quality and clean (Complete by Rob): All three datasets are cleaned, put into the same format, and combined into one file. The script “scripts/clean_data.py” normalizes the data, including names, Wikidata IDs, ULAN IDs, and dates. It also removes placeholder values, impossible dates, anonymous and workshop records, and runs a quality check. The cleaned data then contains 94,509 artist records in “clean/artists_clean.csv”, where 62,571 records are from the Met, 15,949 are from MoMA, and 15,989 are from NGA. Then, the quality results are stored in “results/quality_report.csv”, and “docs/cleaning_and_quality.md" explains each cleaning step and the quality findings. The quality assessment then covered the four dimensions outlined in the plan. The findings for completeness are that with 98% nationality and 88% birth year, NGA is the most complete. Also MoMA has the fewest identifiers with 20% Wikidata and are the only ones with usable gender data (80%). For accuracy, about 3,100 Met artists have guessed date ranges like 1800 to 1900, and some Wikidata IDs are assigned to the wrong person. For consistency, 6,145 artists appear in more than one museum. The museums also disagree on birth year for 12% of them, 9% for death year, and 7% for nationality. For timeliness, the data is current. NGA updates daily and the Met and MoMA update weekly. 
3. Merge the datasets (Design Complete by Peter, Implementation Next): Aligning with our schedule, integration begins after this status report deadline and is on schedule for the end of November. The matching strategy will run on “clean/artists_clean.csv”, where artists will be matched on ULAN IDs and then matched on Wikidata IDs, which were already normalized to the same format during cleaning. Then, names will be matched combined with birth and death years. The similarity threshold will then be chosen during integration from testing different values.
4. Analyze and automate (Not started by Peter and Andy): Once artists are matched, we will measure how many artists show up in one, two, or all three museums. Then we will compare identifier and name based matching along with creating visualizations of the overlap and disagreements between museums. Then, a run-all script will be built that runs every step in order. This will be all the way from downloading the data to the analysis.

## Challenges
Our first challenge was that the Met’s artist ID was wrong. What was happening was that when an artwork had several artists, the Met would combine their ID numbers together to create a larger one. It would also give the same ID to some people, making it very difficult to separate artists from each other. To fix this, we decided to ignore the Met’s IDs and simply identified the artists by their names, plus birth and death years. Our next challenge was that in some of the datasets, missing information was disguised as real values. For example, MoMA would write 0 for unknown years, and the Met would write 9999 for companies that are still in business. The computer kept counting these numbers as real data, which skewed the datasets into being more complete than they actually were. To fix this, we cleaned the dataset and turned all of that missing info into blanks. Our next challenge was that not only did the Met dataset IDs point to different people, but the other dataset, MoMA, would sometimes give two people the exact same Wikidata ID. To address this, identifier matches will be checked against names and birth years during integration, and records where they disagree will be flagged for review. Our next challenge was that NGA’s list included a lot of non-artists. In the NGA dataset, anybody who was connected to artwork, including donors, companies, couples, and funds, was listed. To fix this, we decided to only keep individuals who NGA marked as making an artwork, removing a lot of people from the dataset. Lastly, one of the biggest challenges we have is that data keeps on changing, with NGA updating their files every day, and the Met and MoMA every week. Once the museum updates its file, a new download will get a slightly different file, leading to checksums that will not match. This matters a lot because the project has to be reproducible. Our plan to fix this was to keep a copy of the exact files used and record the download date, which was done in Box.
